A remodel calls for a second unit at the far end of the house, or an all-electric conversion means trading gas for a heat pump model. Every one of them involves new work: gas lines sized for the load, vents routed and pitched properly, circuits matched to the heater, and water lines run cleanly. That is the planning Cambridge Water Heater does before any installation begins.

Before any pipe is cut, we match the heater to your household. For tanks, first-hour rating counts more than gallons; for tankless, it is gallons per minute at your winter temperature rise. Next, we choose the spot together. Gas models require a vent route outdoors and adequate combustion air. Heat pump models require breathing room, a space that stays above roughly 40 degrees, and a place to send condensate. An electric tankless model often has to have its own high-amperage circuit. And wherever a leak could damage finished space, a drain pan goes underneath. Clear choices and a firm price come before any work.

The final stretch is where a careful installer and a fast one part ways. Our technicians leak-test every gas joint, set vent pitch and clearances to the manufacturer's spec, match the circuit and breaker to the heater, and use dielectric unions or fittings where copper meets steel. We pipe the relief valve discharge full size to the right spot, install code-required expansion tanks, drain pans, and seismic straps, and handle the permit if needed. When to Call for Water Heater Installation

Water heaters usually give plenty of warning before they fail, as long as you know what to look and listen for. Keep an eye out for the following:

  • A remodel that moves the utility space to a new spot
  • Venting, relief valve piping, or gas connections that do not look right
  • Gallons wasted down the drain while you wait for hot water
  • An addition too far from the existing heater for quick hot water
  • Plans to change fuel types or go all-electric
  • A tank in a tight spot that is hard to service or replace
  • A new jetted tub that outpaces the heater's capacity
  • A tank that cannot keep up with your household anymore

Why This Job Calls for a Pro

A new water heater installation is really several trades in one job. The gas piping must be sized for the heater plus every other appliance on the line, then pressure-tested until it is leak-free. The vent must meet the manufacturer's rules on diameter, length, pitch, and clearances, or carbon monoxide can find its way indoors. Electric and heat pump units need the right circuit, breaker, and wire gauge, and a whole-house electric tankless unit can demand more current than many panels can spare. On the water side, there is more: shutoffs, dielectric transitions, a correctly rated relief valve with a full-size discharge line, and code items like expansion tanks, drain pans, and seismic straps that vary by location.

What you get from a pro is an installation that is done once. The gas line, vent, and circuit are planned before the first cut, the permit is handled when required, and the relief valve discharge and code items go in correctly. We test every joint, check every gas install for draft and carbon monoxide, and set the temperature low enough to prevent scalding. Hurried installs leave behind undersized gas lines, poor vent pitch, and missing expansion tanks. Ours are done to code and to last. And if the simpler option is the right one for your home, we say so plainly.

How to Prevent Water Heater Problems

A little routine care goes a long way toward keeping a water heater running well past its expected lifespan. Here's where to begin:

  • Flush a tank every six to twelve months to keep sediment from settling
  • Glance at the pan and discharge pipe every month or two; water there means it is time to call
  • Plan an annual descaling if you installed a tankless unit
  • Do not box the heater in; leave room for service and airflow
  • A periodic checkup with an anode inspection keeps a new tank healthy for years
  • Keep flammable liquids and clutter away from gas heaters
  • Keep the temperature around 120 degrees; hotter settings raise the risk of scalding
